# How an NFL offense lines up

**Takeaway.** Every formation is two choices made inside one rule: which five backs, tight ends and receivers are on
the field, and where they stand, with at least seven men on the line.

**Viewer.** A serious fan. Knows the positions, knows shotgun from under center by sight, has heard "eleven
personnel" on a broadcast. Has never been shown how the pieces fit: why the count is always five, why one receiver
stands on the line and another a yard off it, how the names follow from the placement.

**Questions the film answers, in order.**
1. What is the same in every formation?
2. What rule decides where a man may stand?
3. Who are the five players that change, and how are they counted?
4. Where does the quarterback stand?
5. Where do the backs go?
6. Where do the receivers go?
7. How do I read all of it on a real snap?

The `> ` lines are the narration, exactly as it will be spoken. The table under each paragraph is its beat sheet.

## 1. What never changes

> Every formation starts with eleven players. Five interior linemen, who are not eligible receivers. One quarterback. That leaves five: the backs, tight ends and receivers the offense gets to choose.

## 2. The one rule

> One rule shapes where they go. At least seven men on the line at the snap, and only the two on its ends are eligible. So every other receiver stands a yard back.

## 6. Where the receivers go

> Then the receivers. Count each side of the center, tight end included. Two and two is doubles. Three to one side is trips. Squeeze those three together for a bunch. ## 7. Reading a snap

> So read it in two steps. When it substitutes, count the backs and tight ends. That is the personnel, and the defense gets to match it. Then watch where they line up. The same eleven. At least seven on the line. A different picture every snap.

## Sources

- **Eleven players.** 2026 Official Playing Rules of the National Football League, Rule 5, Section 1, Article 1:
  "two teams of 11 players each". https://operations.nfl.com/the-rules/nfl-rulebook/
- **Seven on the line, eligible ends.** Same rulebook, Rule 7, Section 5, Article 1: "(a) It must have seven or more
  players on the line, who may lock legs (3-18)" and "(b) Eligible receivers must be on both ends of the line, and
  all of the players on the line between them must be ineligible receivers."
- **Who is eligible, and the yard back.** Rule 8, Section 1, Article 5: offensive players "on either end of the
  line" and those "legally at least one yard behind the line at the snap", wearing eligible numbers "(0–49 and
  80–89)" or having reported. Article 6(a): ineligible are "Players who are not on either end of their line or at
  least one yard behind it when the ball is snapped". The narration says the interior linemen "are not eligible
  receivers", which is their position's status; a lineman may still catch a pass a defender has touched (Article
  5(d)), and a player may report as eligible (Rule 5, Section 3).
- **Five players left to choose.** Eleven on the field, less five interior linemen and the quarterback. Wikipedia,
  "Personnel grouping (gridiron football)": "The number of wide receivers on the field is implicit, based on the
  fact that eleven offensive players are allowed on the field, including five linemen and the quarterback."
- **Personnel numbers.** First digit running backs, second digit tight ends. Sharp Football Analysis, "NFL Offensive
  Personnel Usage": "The first digit is running backs, the second is tight ends (so '12' is 1 RB and 2 TE)."
  ESPN (Matt Bowen, June 10, 2026): "12 personnel means one running back (the 1), two tight ends (the 2) and two
  wide receivers are on the field."
- **Usage in the 2025 season.** 11 personnel 58.11%, 12 personnel 24.26%, 21 personnel 6.85%, league-wide. PFF, Nic
  Bodiford, "NFL Personnel Trends: How heavier groupings are shrinking the fantasy flex pool", May 27, 2026, table
  "Offensive personnel grouping averages and outliers from 2023-25". These are PFF's charting; the article does not
  say whether playoffs are included. Another charting service (SumerSports) has 12 personnel at 22.0% for the same
  season and agrees on the other two. The film shows whole percentages and credits PFF on screen.
- **Under center, and running either way.** Associated Press, "NFL offenses boost under-center snaps early this
  season, reversing the shotgun trend", September 25, 2026: "With a running back behind an under-center quarterback, offenses can run both
  directions and behind various blocking schemes. With the back standing next to the quarterback in the shotgun,
  some of those options are removed." Bill Barnwell (ESPN, September 17, 2026) says the shotgun alignment "makes the
  side to which they'll ultimately run more predictable".
- **Shotgun, pistol, I formation, singleback, empty.** Wikipedia, "List of formations in American football".
  Shotgun: "The quarterback lines up about five yards behind the center", with a back next to him. Pistol:
  "quarterback lined up closer than in standard shotgun (normally 3 to 4 yards behind center), and a running back
  lined up behind, rather than next to, the QB". I formation: "two backs lined up behind the quarterback", the
  nearer one the fullback. Singleback: "one running back lined up about five yards behind the quarterback. The basic
  singleback set does not employ a fullback." Empty backfield: "all of the backs play near the line of scrimmage to
  act as extra wide receivers or tight ends". The depths are typical values, and the narration says "about".
- **Trips and doubles.** Trips: the same page captions a diagram "Shotgun, Trips left (3 wide receivers on the same
  side)". Doubles is the standard coaching name for the balanced two-by-two set; X&O Labs, "Simplifying Offensive
  Formation Recognition", classes "2x2" as two eligible receivers to each side. The count on each side includes the
  tight end, as coaches count eligible receivers.
- **Bunch and man coverage.** Inside the Pylon glossary, "Bunch Formation" (Mark Schofield, October 31, 2015,
  scoutingacademy.com): "three or more receivers on the same side of an offensive formation, clumped closely
  together", used for "picking off defenders by using crossing routes".
- **The defense gets to match.** Rule 5, Section 2, Article 10: when the offense substitutes, "the offense shall not
  be permitted to snap the ball until the defense has been permitted to respond with its substitutions". Wikipedia,
  "Personnel grouping (gridiron football)": "Defenses are able to substitute players that will allow them to match
  up more favorably against the personnel group that is used by the offense."
- **The diagrams are schematic.** Player spacing on screen is drawn to be readable, not to scale. Each formation on
  screen is checked in the film to have eleven players and exactly seven on the line; the rule allows more than
  seven, and the film does not show that case.
- **Length.** The first full take ran 2:21.7 against a two-minute target: this voice read the script slower than
  the placeholder clock, the reverse of the last film. The two sentences on how often teams line up under center
  (28.6% of snaps in 2023, 42.3% in the first two weeks of 2026; AP with Sportradar data) were cut to bring the
  length back, and because the takeaway does not depend on them.
